Fragrance / essential oil

Vanilla and vanilla-containing fragrances brown over time (the vanillin reacts and darkens the bar) — worth knowing since beer soap already tends to go tan/brown on its own from the malt sugars reacting with lye. Citrus oils smell bright at first but fade fastest in cold process and can speed up trace; pine is sturdier but strong, so start low (5–10 g/kg) and adjust.

General lab safety notes

Summarised from Nikupeteri (2010) chapters 3.4 & 6.1 — the fuller discussion is worth reading directly.

  • Lye is the biggest risk in the room: always add lye to liquid, never liquid to lye, and mix it in a lidded heatproof container so fumes stay contained.
  • Wear gloves, goggles and long sleeves whenever lye or unsaponified ("raw") soap is being handled — raw soap is milder than lye solution but still caustic enough to irritate skin.
  • Weigh oils and lye accurately — a gram or two of error in a small lab-scale batch shifts the superfat percentage a lot more than it would in a large batch.
  • Keep a lidded container or spill mat handy, and know where the nearest eyewash/sink is before you start.
  • Rather than testing cure with a "tongue test" on raw soap (as some older guides suggest), use pH strips — a cured bar should read roughly pH 8–10; anything sharply higher likely still has unreacted lye in it.
  • Fragrance oils belong on the "check before use" list too: only use ones sold for skin/soap use with an available safety data sheet, and watch for oils that accelerate trace or overheat the batch.

Method notes (for the Process log)

Rough temperature and trace expectations for each method, drawn from Nikupeteri (2010) chapter 4.2 and the author's own trial batches — treat as a starting point, since results vary with recipe and equipment.

MethodTypical tempsTrace / notes
Cold processOils and lye solution both around 40–50°C when combinedNo further heating — saponification finishes over the following weeks. Trace can take minutes (stick blender) to over an hour (hand-stirred); insulating the mould speeds up gel.
Hot process — water bathBath at a low boil (~100°C); mass itself around 70–90°CMost even and predictable of the hot methods. Bringing the batter to trace before it goes in the bath makes the cook faster and more reliable.
Hot process — ovenOven at 90–100°CSimilar to a water bath but easier to scorch if the oven runs hot; check every 15–20 minutes.
Hot process — direct heatLow simmer on the stovetopFastest but least predictable — stir constantly and watch for scorching; the batch separates and re-emulsifies a few times before it's done.
Traditional boilingRolling boil for 30–90+ min, then salted outUses lye in excess, then separates finished soap from spent liquid (glycerin, salt, water) by salting. Produces a firmer, more industrial-feeling bar.
Melt & pourMelt base at ~60–70°C (double boiler or microwave)No lye handling — saponification already done in the base. Re-melts and cools quickly, good for layering/swirling.
RebatchGrated soap + a little water, melted at ~70–90°CFor rescuing a batch or reworking a bar — expect a rougher, less glossy finish than a first-generation soap.
